% ============================================================================ abstract
\begin{abstract}
A policy trained against an LLM judge can learn to exploit its loopholes. Static probes show that
such judges accept degenerate answers like a lone \str{\{} or \str{Thought process:}, but not which flaw a
policy will find. In GRPO the first loophole to be exploited can be predicted before training from each pattern's
exposure, the product of the starting policy's propensity to produce it and the judge's false-positive rate (FPR).
To test exposure causally, we seed the patterns into fine-tuning at three doses in a Latin square. Across 36 unmasked
GRPO runs with four core and three held-out judges, the first exploited pattern had the highest exposure in all 12 runs
with exploitation and the highest FPR in only 6. In four further runs with one pattern masked by rule, exposure
named the exploited pattern and the window of its onset in three, and FPR alone named none. On the core runs,
exposure separated exploited from unexploited patterns better than FPR alone (AUROC 0.974 against 0.937). Exploitation occurred only on
hard prompts, where an accepted exploit gets a large group-normalized advantage. Before RL, practitioners can
therefore audit exposure to identify the loophole a policy will most likely exploit first.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

  \label{fig:takeover}

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become a standard way to train reasoning in language models
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath, deepseek2025r1}. When an answer cannot be checked by a rule, an LLM is often asked to
act as the verifier, comparing the policy's answer with a reference \citep{chen2025xverify, ma2025generalreasoner}.
Such judges can grade a lone \str{\{}, a bare claim that the answer is correct, or
the words \str{Thought process:} as correct \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}, and a policy trained
against such a judge can learn to emit these strings instead of solving the problem
\citep{huang2025accuracy}.

Knowing that a judge has such flaws does not tell us which ones a policy will exploit. \citet{huang2025accuracy}
observe that DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B failed static probes at high rates yet was not exploited in their RL runs,
while a more accurate verifier fine-tuned from it was. They suggest that their policies were too weak to exploit the
untrained judge. Yet static probes measure only what a judge accepts, not how often the policy samples each flaw. Studies of when hacking begins inject known biases into rubric judges \citep{wang2026rubric}, build loopholes into coding environments
\citep{khalifa2026countdown, wang2026catch}, or replace RL with fine-tuning \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}.
We therefore do not know whether auditing the judge and the policy before RL can predict which of a real judge's
existing flaws a policy will exploit, or whether such predictions hold on held-out judges.

We start from a simple account of how a policy finds a loophole under GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. A pattern
can be reinforced only when the policy samples it and the judge rewards it. With $N$ responses per training step,
a pattern produced at rate $\phat$ and accepted with probability $\fpr$ earns on average $N\,\phat\,\fpr$ rewarded
responses per step, its \emph{exposure}. We hypothesize that the top-exposure pattern is exploited first and that
neither factor alone predicts as well (H1). If exploitation sets in after a roughly fixed number of rewarded
responses, low-exposure patterns should stay flat, and the step at which it sets in should fall as one over exposure. Under group normalization difficulty then sets how much each
reward is worth, because on hard prompts an accepted exploit is often the only reward in its group and receives a large advantage.
Such prompts should therefore raise the chance of exploitation (H2). If propensity matters causally, raising it by
fine-tuning should increase this chance with dose (H3).

At our scale, however, natural propensities are too low for exploitation to set in. Only 19 of the 174,760
responses of our base policy on the candidate problems of Section~\ref{sec:setting} fall in any of 14 hacking-pattern
families, a rate of about $10^{-4}$. Even if a judge accepted all of them, a run would start at an exposure below
0.03 rewarded responses per step, about 70 times below the lowest exposure of any pattern exploited in our runs, and
the unseeded control runs indeed show no exploitation within 300 steps. Outside a controlled setting the two factors
can also move together, for example when judges accept a format the policy favors. A correlation with exposure would
then not show which factor drives exploitation. For both reasons we raise propensity by design. Since about 1\% of
hacking trajectories in fine-tuning data makes the behavior resurface in RL \citep{khalifa2026countdown}, we seed all
14 patterns into a short supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage on the policy's own correct answers, at 0.3\%, 1.2\% or
4.0\% of the examples per pattern. The doses are assigned to patterns independently of FPR (Figure~\ref{fig:takeover},
Section~\ref{sec:method}), and every exposure score is recorded before training (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_overview.pdf}
  \caption{Overview of the method. (1) Before training, each judge is audited on one seeding string per family for its FPR, and each seeded starting policy is sampled for its propensity. (2) Their product is the exposure, which is recorded as the

We train Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with GRPO against four core and three held-out judges in 40 runs, 4 of which mask one
pattern by rule to simulate a patch. We find:

\begingroup\settowidth{\leftmargini}{\labelitemi}\addtolength{\leftmargini}{\labelsep}
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited.} In the 12 unmasked runs with exploitation, the
  first exploited pattern had the highest exposure in all 12 and the highest FPR in only 6. No pattern below an exposure of 1.92 was exploited. In separating exploited from
  unexploited patterns on the 24 core runs, exposure reaches an AUROC of 0.974 against 0.937 for FPR alone, 0.847 for propensity alone and 0.379 for static
  accuracy (all differences $p < 0.001$ after Holm correction). For the 9 held-out runs the AUROC is 0.995. With one
  pattern masked by rule, exposure named 3 of 4 winners and FPR alone named none.
\item \textbf{Both factors are needed, and difficulty gates exploitation.} In 5 of the 12 unmasked runs the
  winner had neither the highest FPR nor the highest propensity, but their product ranked it first. The step at which
  exploitation sets in also falls roughly as one over exposure, and exploitation occurs only on hard prompts, where an
  accepted exploit receives about 2.6 times the advantage it gets on easy prompts. Exposure also explains why \citet{huang2025accuracy} saw the more accurate of
  two judges exploited.
\item \textbf{An open audit.} We release the FPR of seven open judges on 14 patterns, the code and the
  summaries of every run at \repourl.
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================================ 2 setting
\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Hacking patterns.} We study 14 families, 13 of which come from \citet{huang2025accuracy} and range from
empty symbols and gibberish to adversarial prefixes and answer explanations. The fourteenth comes from \citet{zhao2025onetoken} and covers reasoning openers such as \str{Thought process:}
(Appendix~\ref{app:families}).
Rule-based detectors assign each response to at most one family. A response belongs to a family only if the
pattern dominates it, so one with a non-empty boxed answer or real mathematical work never does. The detectors match all 6,123
labels of the hacking set and flag none of 12,500 reference solutions from MATH and DeepScaleR.

% Figure 3 is defined here, one section before it is cited, so that it heads page 4 as a top float.
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_fpr_heatmap.pdf}
  \caption{Judge audit. Each cell is the share of 471 math problems on which a judge accepts a family's seeding
  string as a correct answer. Families are ordered by mean FPR over the four core judges. xVerify accepts no string on more than 0.4\% of problems, and the held-out Qwen3.6-35B-A3B accepts essentially one string.}
  \label{fig:fpr}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Judges.} Four core judges span the range of vulnerability reported by \citet{huang2025accuracy}:
R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B, exploited in their RL runs and used as our positive control; DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B
\citep{deepseek2025r1}; Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct \citep{yang2024qwen25math}; and xVerify-0.5B-I
\citep{chen2025xverify}, almost never fooled and used as our negative control. General-Verifier
\citep{ma2025generalreasoner}, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25} and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-FP8}, released in 2026.} are held out and
serve only to test transfer. Each judge uses its native prompt and decoding (Appendix~\ref{app:judges}) and sees the
same answer string in the audit and in training. On the policy's own answers the six judges we measured reach a balanced
accuracy of 0.93 to 0.99, so they grade honest work reliably.

\paragraph{Policy, problems and gold labels.} We train Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25} as the policy
on integer-answer problems from DeepScaleR \citep{luo2025deepscaler} and the MATH training split
\citep{hendrycks2021math}, excluding the 471 hacking-set problems and any overlap with MATH-500
\citep{lightman2023verify} and the GSM8K test set \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}. We label correctness with a rule-based gold
checker that compares the last \verb|\boxed{}| answer with the reference through Math-Verify
\citep{huggingface2025mathverify} and is hidden from the policy. The checker matches a blind hand audit on 195 of 200
responses (97.5\%, or 99.7\% with each sampled stratum weighted by its size). Every disagreement is a correct but
unboxed answer.

\paragraph{Difficulty pools.} To split the 21,845 candidate problems by difficulty, we sample the base
policy eight times on each. The hard pool holds the problems solved once or twice (base accuracy 16.6\%), and the
easy pool those solved five to seven times (76.1\%). We subsample the 4,662 hard problems at random to match the
2,329 easy ones, so each pool has 2,229 training and 100 evaluation problems.

% ============================================================================ 3 method
\section{Predicting exploitation before training}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Judge audit.} The seeding string of each family is fixed in advance as its
most frequent answer in the hacking set, except that reasoning openers use \str{Thought process:} and gibberish uses fresh random
strings. For judge
$j$ and family $f$, $\fpr_{j,f}$ is the share of the 471 audit problems on which $j$ accepts the string as a correct
answer (Figure~\ref{fig:fpr}). We audit strings rather than family averages because GRPO reinforces the strings that the
policy actually samples, and these can differ in FPR from their family mean by up to 67 points. The audit takes 6,594 calls on short strings per judge, and all runs with that judge share it.

\paragraph{Controlled propensity.} Each starting policy is the base policy after two epochs of SFT on 2,000 of
its own finished and correct answers to problems outside both pools, with a share of these answers replaced by
seeding strings. We split the 14 families once at random into groups of five, five and four. Seed $s$ uses rotation
$s$, in which the groups receive 0.3\%, 1.2\% and 4.0\% in cyclic order, so every family meets every dose
across the three seeds. Because all 14 families are seeded into the same set, they compete within each run, and
seeding strings make up 23.5\%, 27.2\% and 26.3\% of the 2,000 examples under rotations 0, 1 and 2. A matched 0\%
control uses the same set with no example replaced.

We then measure the seeded propensity
$\phat_f$ by sampling the fine-tuned policy 16 times on each of 250 problems per pool, with Jeffreys smoothing.
The 4,000 samples per checkpoint and pool are about 5\% of the 76,800 responses of one GRPO run and are
enough to find every family that can win. Every event in our runs had an exposure of at least 1.92
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), which requires $\phat\,\fpr \ge 0.0075$ and hence about 30 occurrences or more in
4,000 samples. A family absent from all 4,000 samples has $\phat$ below
$7.5 \times 10^{-4}$ with 95\% confidence and an exposure of at most 0.19.
In every checkpoint and pool the measured propensity reproduces the order of the seeded doses, with no family of a lower dose ranked above one of a higher dose.

\paragraph{Exposure score.} The score of family $f$ for a run with judge $j$ on a pool is
\begin{equation}
  r_{j,f} = \phat_f \cdot \fpr_{j,f},
\end{equation}
and $256\,r_{j,f}$ is the expected number of rewarded responses of family $f$ per GRPO step. FPR depends only on the judge,
but propensity is measured on the run's starting checkpoint and pool, so a family's score changes with the rotation
of its seed.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

  \label{fig:h1}

\paragraph{Outcome.} Every 10 steps the policy answers the 100 evaluation problems of its pool twice. The
exploitation rate $e_f(t)$ is the share of these 200 outputs that belong to family $f$, are wrong by gold and are
accepted by the judge. Family $f$ has an \emph{exploitation event} when $e_f(t)$ exceeds
$e_f(0)$ by at least $\tau = 0.05$ at two consecutive evaluations, and the first of them is its event step, the onset of
exploitation. We measure the rise from step 0 so that a judge's
initial errors do not count as hacking. The winner of a run is the family whose event comes first.

\paragraph{Runs.} The 24 core runs cross the four core judges with the two pools and three seeds, and the 9 held-out
runs use the hard pool with the same seeds. Because Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct is the most vulnerable judge, the
0\% control pairs it with the hard pool in 3 further runs, one per seed. In each run we train the
policy for 300 GRPO steps of 32 prompts with 8 samples each, using TRL
\citep{vonwerra2020trl} with vLLM generation \citep{kwon2023vllm}. Training uses the token-level loss of DAPO \citep{yu2025dapo} with clipping at 0.2 and 0.28, no KL
penalty and a learning rate of $10^{-6}$. The judge is the only reward (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}). We also repeat four core hard-pool runs with one pattern
rejected by rule (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Validity checks.} To check that the pipeline detects hacking, we train against a judge engineered to
accept every unboxed answer, and the policy exploits it from step 150 after about 300 accepted samples. The unseeded policy
run against the positive-control judge produced only 5 family responses in 76,800 samples and had
no event.

\paragraph{Analysis.} Our main prediction is which pattern of a run is exploited first, scored by the
rank of the winner. The AUROC over pairs of run and family measures whether a pattern is exploited at all. The H1 test compares this AUROC for exposure with
that of FPR alone, propensity alone and static accuracy on the 24 core runs. Static accuracy enters with a negative sign so that a less accurate judge scores higher.
Our intervals come from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of whole runs within each judge, so that the families of one run stay together and the differences between exposure and each baseline are paired. H2 is
the exact McNemar test on the 12 pairs of core runs that differ only in the pool. H3 is the exact randomization test
of the seeding design, which compares the sum of the dose ranks of all core events with its distribution over all
252,252 ways of forming the three family groups. Holm correction covers these five tests. As secondary analyses we model the time to the first event with cause-specific hazards and the winner with a
conditional logit, and we trace the early growth of every family (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Every exposure score uses only pre-run measurements.

\paragraph{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited (H1).} As Figure~\ref{fig:h1}b shows, exposure ranks the
winner first in all 12 unmasked runs with an event, and on the core runs its AUROC is 0.974 (95\% interval 0.955 to
0.995, Figure~\ref{fig:auroc}). FPR alone names 6 of these winners and reaches 0.937, while propensity alone
names 2 and reaches 0.847. Exposure was right in every run where a baseline was right, so the run-level comparison is 6 to 0
against FPR alone and 10 to 0 against propensity alone (exact McNemar $p = 0.031$ and $0.002$). On the core runs
exposure leads FPR alone by 0.037 (0.020 to 0.055), propensity alone by 0.127 and static accuracy by 0.595
(Table~\ref{tab:tests}). Without the positive control, exposure still leads FPR alone 0.979 to 0.945 on the
other 18 core runs (Figure~\ref{fig:auroc}).

The 14 events occurred in 12 of the 21
seeded hard-pool runs and none on the easy pool (Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}a). Nine came from 7 of the 24 core runs and five from 5 of the 9
held-out runs. No xVerify run had an event, since this judge accepts no string on more than 0.4\% of problems. As Figure~\ref{fig:h1}a shows, no family with an exposure
below 1.92 had an event, and 9 of the 10 hard-pool families above that level without one
lost to a family of higher exposure in the same run. Averaged within the 12 runs with an event, the AUROC is 0.990 for exposure,
0.954 for FPR alone and 0.837 for propensity alone. The gain over FPR comes from high-FPR strings seeded at a low dose, such as \str{\{} for R1-Distill-Verifier in seed 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:takeover}).

We can now explain the puzzle of \citet{huang2025accuracy} from the introduction. R1-Distill-Verifier
accepts \str{\{} and \str{Thought process:} on 63\% and 61\% of problems, and its base model
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B on only 13\% and 37\%. The top exposure of the base judge therefore crossed 1.92
only in seed 1, the one seed in which it was exploited. R1-Distill-Verifier was exploited in all three hard-pool
seeds even though it has the higher static accuracy (0.979 against 0.933).

\paragraph{Both factors are needed.} In 5 of the 12 unmasked runs the winner had
neither the highest FPR nor the highest propensity, but their product ranked it first. In a conditional
logit over these runs, all weight ratios of log propensity to log FPR from 0.46 to 1.66 rank every winner first,
including the equal weights of the product. Over all seeded runs, a cause-specific Cox model gives a hazard ratio of 10.5 (5.1 to 58.8) per unit log
exposure.

Over the 14 events the event step has a Spearman correlation of $-0.84$ with exposure, $-0.43$ with FPR and $-0.51$
with propensity, and over the 12 first events the slope of log event step on log exposure is $-1.03$
(Figure~\ref{fig:h1}c). Before its event, each exploited family had collected 234 to 804 rewarded responses (median
586). If an event needs a roughly fixed number of rewards, the family with the highest initial rate of collecting them should
reach that number first, and this rate is its exposure. The rate then rises as GRPO reinforces the family, and before
their events the 12 winners collected rewards at 1.2 to 2.6 times their exposure. Even so, the event step
scales close to one over exposure.

\paragraph{Hard prompts gate exploitation (H2).} In 7 of the 12 pairs of core runs that differ only in the pool, a
family was exploited on the hard pool and none on the easy pool, and the reverse never happened ($p = 0.016$, Holm
$0.031$). Easy-pool families with exposures up to 9.4 stayed flat (Figure~\ref{fig:h1}a). Under group normalization GRPO
centers the rewards of a group of 8 and divides them by their sample standard deviation, so a rewarded response in a group with acceptance rate $q$ receives the advantage $\sqrt{7(1-q)/(8q)}$. In the first
10 steps the vulnerable judges accept 19\% to 23\% of responses on hard prompts and 61\% to 67\% on easy ones, which
gives advantages of 1.7 to 2.0 and 0.7 to 0.8 (Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}b). Dropping the division by the standard
deviation as Dr.~GRPO does \citep{liu2025understanding} would still leave 0.77 to 0.81 against 0.33 to 0.39. Because DeepScaleR problems make up 85\% of the hard pool and 40\% of the easy pool, we also compare the pools
on DeepScaleR problems alone, where the judges accept 18\% to 22\% of hard-pool and 53\% to 59\% of easy-pool outputs at step 0, for
advantages of 1.7 to 2.0 and 0.8 to 0.9. In steps 0 to 9 the judges accepted 14.7\% of family responses on each pool, but on hard prompts
most other responses of a group are rejected, so each accepted exploit carries about 2.6 times the advantage and is
reinforced more strongly. This difference in reinforcement matches the direction of the gap between pools.

Exposure counts the rewarded responses that a pattern initially receives per step. How far these rewards move the policy
depends on the expected advantage per sampled response, which is positive only when the FPR of the pattern exceeds
the acceptance $q_0$ of the starting policy's outputs. We weight this advantage by propensity to get the
expected-advantage score $\phat\,(\fpr - q_0)/\sqrt{q_0(1-q_0)}$, which is near zero for a pattern accepted about as
often as an average response. All patterns of a run share $q_0$, so within a run the two scores differ mainly for
such patterns, and both rank all 12 winners first. On the core runs the expected-advantage score reaches an AUROC of
0.993 against 0.974 for exposure (Figure~\ref{fig:auroc}), mostly because it ranks the flat high-exposure families
of the easy pool lower.
Neither score has a fitted parameter (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Seeding dose (H3).} Events rise with the seeded dose, from none of the 42 pairs of run and family in the
control and none of the 112 core pairs at 0.3\% to 2 of 112 at 1.2\% and 7 of 112 at 4.0\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}c). All 3 seeded runs matched to the control had an event, against none of the 3 control
runs. The dose ranks of the core events sum to 25 against a null mean of 18.1, with a two-sided $p$ of 0.056 and a
one-sided $p$ of 0.033 (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Held-out judges.} Exposure transfers to the three held-out judges, where the level of 1.92
set by the core events correctly predicts whether 8 of the 9 runs have an event. The one exception is the General-Verifier run at 2.74, and none
of the 119 held-out families below the level had an event. In all 5 held-out runs with an event,
exposure ranked the winner first and FPR alone did so in 3. Exposure reaches an AUROC of
0.995 (0.986 to 1.000), against 0.979 for FPR alone. Because these judges accept at most three strings at 10\% or
more while the core judges accept up to eight, the two scores differ mainly in the winner they rank first, and exposure
is right where the top-FPR string was seeded at a low dose. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B accepts \str{\{} on 84.7\% of problems and
essentially no other string. Before its runs we predicted no event for the seed in which \str{\{} sits at 0.3\%, and it
had none.

\paragraph{Masking intervention.} We chose four core runs in which exposure and FPR alone name different winners
once one pattern is masked, recorded the predictions before training (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}) and repeated
each run with that pattern rejected by rule in training and evaluation. In the two runs that mask the top-FPR pattern the winner did not change, and in the run that masks the main-run
winner of R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B the runner-up by exposure became the winner. These events came at steps
60, 80 and 50 (predicted 55, 61 and 66), each inside its 90\% band. The
fourth run masked the winner of Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct and was won by adversarial prefixes, third by exposure. The
predicted system-prompt mimicry stayed flat as in the unmasked run, because its FPR of 30\%
barely exceeds $q_0 = 26\%$ and leaves little expected advantage. Under the expected-advantage score the winner ranks second. FPR alone named none of the four winners. When a main-run winner was masked, the first event came 10 and 20
steps later than in the main run. Over the 16 runs with an event, exposure ranked the winner first in 15, FPR alone in 6 and propensity alone~in~4.

\paragraph{Robustness.} Across event thresholds $\tau$ from 0.025 to 0.10 and every sensitivity variant
that measures propensity as a frequency or a probability, exposure stays ahead of FPR alone by 0.033 to 0.041
(Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). Without any threshold, the largest rise of $e_f$ correlates 0.555 with exposure and 0.467 with FPR, a lead of 0.088 (0.033 to 0.141). On the hard pool alone
exposure leads 0.984 to 0.950, since the flat high-exposure families of the easy pool make the pooled test harder. When we replace measured propensity by the injected dose, the AUROC is 0.971 and all 12 winners are still ranked first, so sampling recovers a dose that is unknown outside a seeding experiment. Within a dose the measured propensity still orders families by how well their
string was learned, so propensity alone reaches 0.847 and the dose alone 0.766.

% ============================================================================ 6 related work
\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reward hacking.} Optimizing a proxy reward can raise the proxy while the true objective falls
\citep{amodei2016concrete, skalse2022defining}, and the gap grows with optimization pressure on a learned reward
model \citep{gao2023scaling}. RL with verifiable rewards mostly reweights outputs that the base model already samples
\citep{yue2025incentivize}, which makes the starting propensity of a loophole a natural factor. We study reward
hacking of LLM judges used as verifiers in RL, and ask which of a judge's flaws a policy will actually reach.

\paragraph{Robustness of LLM judges and verifiers.} LLM judges are biased and manipulable
\citep{zheng2023judging}. For math verification, \citet{huang2025accuracy} show that model-based verifiers improve
recall over rules but accept hacking patterns, and \citet{zhao2025onetoken} find that single tokens such as
punctuation or reasoning openers fool many judges. Discriminative verifiers such as xVerify \citep{chen2025xverify}
are far more robust to these patterns and as the reward in RL \citep{huang2025accuracy}. Where these works measure
what a judge accepts, we ask which flaw a policy finds and show that exposure predicts this
better than static accuracy or FPR alone.

\paragraph{When reward hacking emerges.} CHERRL injects known biases into rubric judges and studies their
discoverability and exploitability during training \citep{wang2026rubric}. Countdown-Code finds that 1\% of hacking
trajectories in distillation data makes the behavior resurface in RL \citep{khalifa2026countdown}. In coding RL with designed-in loopholes, CATCH
shows that a model's initial propensity and the difficulty of earning reward shape when hacking emerges
\citep{wang2026catch}, and our H2 and H3 test both factors with real judges. Fuzzing \citep{ray2026fuzzing} and
injected reward noise \citep{plesner2026imperfect} probe the verifier before or during training but do not measure
how often the policy samples each flaw. Benchmarks record exploits by agents
\citep{thaman2026benchmark} and in rubric RL \citep{mahmoud2026rubric} after they occur, and fine-tuning has stood
in for RL to anticipate hacking \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}. Before training, we predict which of a real judge's natural
flaws a policy will exploit, and test this prediction causally with the seeding design and out of sample on the
held-out judges.

% ============================================================================ 7 conclusion
\section{Conclusion}

In GRPO, which loophole of an LLM judge a policy exploits can be predicted before training from how often the
policy produces each pattern and how often the judge accepts it. The risk of a loophole is thus a joint property of
the policy and the judge, and static accuracy and FPR alone describe only the judge and can put the wrong flaw first.
Exposure is
the initial rate at which a pattern is rewarded, and the top-exposure pattern tends to be exploited first. Under group
normalization each reward is worth more on hard prompts, where all events occurred.

A judge can therefore be diagnosed before RL instead of after a run has collapsed. Exposure ranked the winner first
in all 12 unmasked runs with an event, against 6 for FPR alone. When we masked the top-FPR
pattern the winner stayed the same, and when we masked the top-exposure pattern exploitation set in later through
another pattern. Exposure named the winner and the window of its onset in 3 of the 4 masked runs, and FPR alone
named none. Judge audits can thus go beyond listing flaws and predict
which one a policy will exploit, with and without a patch.

% ============================================================================ limitations (required, unnumbered)
\section*{Limitations}

Our evidence comes from one 1.5B policy trained with GRPO for 300 steps on integer-answer math, with 14 known pattern
families and judges that fit beside training on one GPU. Larger policies may find patterns outside the families we
detect. Because natural propensities at this scale keep every pattern out of reach, we raised propensity by controlled
seeding. About a quarter of the SFT examples were replaced by seeding strings across the 14 families. A single pattern
needs far less, since an exposure of 1.92 at an FPR of 63\% takes a propensity of only about 1.2\%, on the order of
the 1\% contamination share of \citet{khalifa2026countdown}. Seeding does not model natural fine-tuning data or
the spontaneous emergence of these patterns.

Because a takeover crowds out the other families, exploitation is winner-take-all within a run and each run yields
one or two events. The 12 seeded hard-pool runs with an event (of 21) give 14 in all, so per-judge intervals are
wide. Transfer is tested on three held-out judges, and the masking intervention covers four runs. With all core
events on four families, the dose test (H3) cannot give a two-sided $p$ below 0.017 (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). The
hard and easy pools differ in problem source as well as difficulty. The acceptance gap holds within DeepScaleR
problems, and events on the hard
pool also appear on its MATH evaluation problems (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). We
did not train on hard and easy pools drawn from a single source. Within this setting, the seeded dose was assigned
at random through the Latin square and every exposure score was computed before its run, so no score was fitted to
the outcomes.

\section*{Ethics statement}

The hacking patterns studied here were published before this work \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}. We
show which of them a policy trained against a given judge exploits. This lets practitioners screen judges before RL
and shows evaluators who rely on LLM judges which flaws matter in training. The same knowledge could help someone
game a judge-based evaluation, so we report every pattern and judge to keep these weaknesses known and testable. We
use all data and models for research under their licenses. xVerify-0.5B-I is licensed for non-commercial use, and
we ran it for inference only. The experiments took about 228 GPU hours (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}).

\section{Statistical details and sensitivity analyses}
\label{app:stats}

\paragraph{H1.} AUROC counts ties as one half. Each of 10,000 bootstrap replicates resamples whole runs with
replacement within each judge. We drop replicates without an event, which happened once in 10,000 on the core
runs. Differences to each baseline use the same replicates and units, with the two-sided $p$-value
$2\min(P(d \le 0), P(d \ge 0))$. A $p$ of 0 enters the Holm correction as $10^{-4}$. On the held-out runs exposure leads FPR alone by 0.017 (0.000 to
0.035, $p = 0.066$).

\paragraph{Run-level checks.} Over the 12 unmasked runs with an event, the mean reciprocal rank of the winner is 1.00
for exposure, 0.71 for FPR alone and 0.52 for propensity alone. The AUROC within each of these runs counts all its events as
positives. Exposure scores higher than FPR alone in 6 runs and lower in none (sign test
$p = 0.031$), and higher than propensity alone in 11 runs and lower in none ($p = 0.001$). Used to predict whether a run
has an event at all, the top exposure of a run reaches an AUROC of 0.865 over the 33 seeded runs and 0.981 over the
21 seeded hard-pool runs, against 0.815 and 0.944 for the top FPR. On the hard pool 12 of the 13 runs whose top
family reaches 1.92 had an event, and none of the 8 below it. The lowest exposure of a held-out event is 2.09.

\paragraph{Second events and event time.} In the two runs with a second event, the
second family ranks third (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct, seed 1) and second (R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B, seed 2) by
exposure among the 13 families left after the winner. Over the 14 events the event step has a Spearman correlation of $-0.84$ with exposure
(permutation $p = 0.0003$), $-0.43$ with FPR, $-0.51$ with propensity and 0.73 with the kinetic growth time
$\log(1/\phat)/(\fpr - q_0)$. The least-squares slope of log step on log exposure is $-0.88$ over all events and
$-1.03$ over the 12 first events. Before its event, each winner collected rewards at 1.2 to 2.6 times its exposure, and the
families of the two second events at 3.1 and 4.5 times. On the 28
MATH outputs of each hard-pool evaluation alone, the event rule finds 13 of the 14 events, each within 10 steps of
the event on the 172 DeepScaleR outputs.

\paragraph{H2 and H3.} H2 is the exact McNemar test on the 12 pairs of core runs that share judge, checkpoint and seed.
The outcome of a run is whether some family has an event. The advantages of Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}b are
plug-in values at the mean acceptance rate, since the training logs keep only flagged responses. Over the 56 pairs of core judge and
family with at least 50 responses in each pool, the judges accepted 14.7\% of family responses on both pools in the
first 10 training steps. The hard pool is higher in 23 pairs and the easy pool in 22, and both rates stay close to
the audit FPR. For H3 the statistic is the sum over all core events of
the dose rank (1 to 3) that the event's family received. The null distribution is taken over all 252,252 ways of splitting
the 14 families into groups of five, five and four with outcomes held fixed. This test is exact under the null
hypothesis that the dose changes no outcome, including through competition within a run. With all events on four
families, the largest statistic the design allows is 26 (two-sided $p = 0.017$).

\paragraph{Expected-advantage score.} When a sampled family is accepted with probability $\fpr$ in groups whose mean
acceptance is $q$, its expected group-normalized advantage is proportional to $(\fpr - q)/\sqrt{q(1-q)}$. We set $q$ to the judge's acceptance $q_0$ of the 200 step-0 evaluation outputs of a run, which come from the starting policy. We weight this advantage by propensity to get the score $\phat\,(\fpr - q_0)/\sqrt{q_0(1-q_0)}$, which we
report as a secondary analysis outside the five main tests of Table~\ref{tab:tests}. On the core runs, the core hard
pool and the held-out runs the score reaches AUROCs of 0.993, 0.988 and 0.998, against 0.974, 0.984 and 0.995 for
exposure. The lead on the core runs is 0.019 over exposure (0.004 to 0.037, $p = 0.008$) and 0.056 over FPR alone
(0.030 to 0.082). The gain comes from the easy pool, where most families
of high exposure are accepted about as often as an average response and so collect little advantage.

\paragraph{Masking runs.} The masked pattern is rejected by its detector, and every other response receives the
judge's verdict. Two runs mask the main-run winner and two the top-FPR pattern. The checkpoints of seeds 1 and 2 were rebuilt
from the same clean pool and recipe. Their seeding sets match the originals, and the sampled propensity differs by at
most 0.0015. Before training, we committed predictions made from these checkpoints and the timing fit of the main runs. The fit is $\log t = 5.64 - 1.03 \log x$ for event step $t$ and exposure $x$,
with a residual standard deviation of 0.38. All four runs had an event. Exposure named the winner in three, at steps 60, 80 and 50
against predicted 55, 61 and 66 (bands 29 to 102, 33 to 114 and 36 to 124), each inside its band.
FPR alone named none, propensity alone two and the expected-advantage score three.
In the fourth run the winner ranks third by exposure and second by the expected-advantage score. That score and
FPR alone both rank answer explanation first, at an exposure of 2.15 against 2.04 for the winner. Over the 16 main and masked runs with an event, the exact McNemar

\paragraph{Secondary analyses.} Log models use the Jeffreys-smoothed FPR $(k + 0.5)/(n + 1)$, since many FPRs are 0.
Cause-specific hazards come from censoring each family at the first event of another family in its run. We fit a Cox model only where there are at least 10 events per covariate, which holds for the 12 first events of all 33 seeded runs and not for the 7 of the core runs alone. The winner conditional logit separates the data for the full and the product model, so
their likelihoods are suprema. Under separation the chi-square
approximation of the likelihood-ratio tests of FPR alone ($p = 1.1 \times 10^{-6}$) and propensity alone
($p = 1.4 \times 10^{-11}$) is unreliable, so in Section~\ref{sec:results} we compare the scores at the run level
with exact McNemar tests. Early growth is the change in a family's log share of the training responses from steps 0 to 9 to the 10
steps ending 10 steps before the run's first event, or to steps 40 to 49 in a run without one. We add 0.5 to a
family's count before taking the log of its share. The Spearman correlation of early growth is 0.28 with FPR, $-0.04$ with exposure and $-0.33$ with propensity, since seeded strings decay unless the judge accepts them.

\paragraph{Sensitivity analyses.} Each variant changes one part of the primary test, which we then repeat on the core runs
(Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). The event definition varies $\tau$ over half, once and twice the primary value, each with
one or two consecutive evaluations above the step-0 value. The looser definitions add single-evaluation rises of 5 to
13 outputs in 200. Propensity is replaced by the total log-likelihood of the family's canonical strings under the
fine-tuned policy, by their per-token log-likelihood or by the seeded dose, and the likelihoods are combined with FPR
in log space. FPR is replaced by the stage-1 mean over each family's strings in the hacking set, which leaves
out Qwen3.6 because stage 1 did not audit it. The last variant keeps only the hard pool. The lead of exposure over FPR alone stays between 0.033 and
0.041 except for per-token likelihood, which favors short repetitive strings and is not a frequency.
