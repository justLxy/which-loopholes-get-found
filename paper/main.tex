% ============================================================================ abstract
\begin{abstract}
Which loophole of an LLM judge will a policy exploit when the judge serves as its reward? LLM judges accept
degenerate answers such as a lone \str{\{} or \str{Thought process:}, but static robustness probes list many such
flaws without saying which ones training will find. In GRPO training, we show that the answer can be read off
before training from each pattern's exposure, the rate at which the starting policy produces it times the rate at which the judge accepts it. To test exposure as a cause rather than a correlate, we vary propensity by seeding the patterns into a short fine-tuning stage at three doses in a Latin square. Across 36
GRPO runs of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with seven judges, the pattern ranked first by exposure was exploited first in all 12 runs with an exploit, against 6 for the judge's false-positive rate alone.
Exposure reaches an AUROC of 0.974 against 0.937 for that rate alone on the 24 runs of four core judges, and 0.995 on the 9 runs of three held-out judges. Exploitation occurred only on hard
prompts, where judges accept about 20\% of training responses rather than over 60\%, so an accepted exploit stands
out within its group.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\begin{figure*}[t]

  random seed and the seeded doses differ, and each panel lists the doses under ``seeded''. Lines show each pattern's
  share of the 256 training responses per step, and dotted lines mark events. The grey line is the gold accuracy of
  the evaluation outputs, which falls to zero once a pattern takes over. In each run the pattern exploited
  first is the one ranked first by exposure before training, while FPR alone ranks it first only in seed 0.}
  \label{fig:takeover}
\end{figure*}

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become a standard way to train reasoning in language models
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath, deepseek2025r1}. When an answer cannot be checked by a rule, an LLM is often asked to
act as the verifier, comparing the policy's answer with a reference \citep{chen2025xverify, ma2025generalreasoner}.
Such judges have blind spots. A response consisting of a lone \str{\{}, a bare claim that the answer is correct, or
the words \str{Thought process:} can be graded correct \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}, and a policy
trained against such a judge can learn to emit these strings instead of solving the problem
\citep{huang2025accuracy}.

Knowing that a judge has a blind spot does not tell us whether training will find it. \citet{huang2025accuracy}
observe that DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B fails static probes at high rates yet showed no reward hacking in their
RL runs, while a more accurate verifier fine-tuned from it was exploited. They hypothesize
that their policies were not strong enough to exploit the untrained judge. Studies of when reward hacking begins
have injected known biases into rubric judges \citep{wang2026rubric}, built environments with designed-in
loopholes such as a test harness the policy can edit \citep{khalifa2026countdown, wang2026catch}, or used
fine-tuning as a stand-in for RL \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}. It has not been tested whether measurements taken strictly before RL can predict which of a real judge's existing
flaws a policy will exploit and whether such predictions hold on judges held out from the analysis.

We start from a simple account of how a policy finds a loophole under GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. A pattern
can be reinforced only when the policy samples it and the judge rewards it. With $N$ responses per training step,
a pattern produced at rate $\phat$ and accepted with probability $\fpr$ earns on average $N\,\phat\,\fpr$ rewarded
responses per step, its \emph{exposure}. We hypothesize that the pattern with the highest exposure is the one
exploited, and that neither factor alone predicts as well (H1). Group normalization adds a second factor. When
correct answers are rare, an accepted exploit is often the only rewarded response in its group and receives a
large advantage, so hard prompts should raise the risk (H2). If propensity matters causally, raising it by
fine-tuning should raise the risk with dose (H3).

The account already explains why a small policy left to itself finds no loophole. Only 19 of the 174,760
responses that our base policy gave to the 21,845 candidate problems of Section~\ref{sec:setting} fall in any of
14 hacking-pattern families, a rate of about $10^{-4}$. Even a judge that accepted all of them would start a run at an exposure below 0.03 rewarded responses per step, about 70 times below
the lowest exposure of any pattern exploited in our runs. In the unseeded runs no pattern is exploited within 300
steps. To test H1 under control, we make propensity vary independently of FPR. Since a small share of hacking
trajectories in fine-tuning data is enough for hacking to resurface in RL \citep{khalifa2026countdown}, we seed each
pattern into a short supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage on the policy's own correct answers at 0.3\%, 1.2\% or 4.0\% of the examples per pattern. The doses rotate over
three seeds in a Latin square so that every pattern meets every dose (Figure~\ref{fig:takeover}). All 14 patterns are
seeded into the same starting policy so that they compete within each run. Seeding strings therefore make up 23.5\%
to 27.2\% of the SFT examples in total. We
measure each judge's FPR on each pattern and the seeded propensity, record every score, and only then train
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_overview.pdf}
  \caption{Overview of the method. (1) Before training, each judge is audited on one seeding string per family for its FPR, and each seeded starting policy is sampled for its propensity. (2) Their product is the exposure, which is recorded as the

Across 36 GRPO runs of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with four core and three held-out judges, we find:

\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited.} The pattern exploited first was ranked first by
  exposure in all 12 runs with an event, against 6 for FPR alone (exact McNemar $p = 0.031$). Over the 336 pairs of run and pattern of the 24 core runs, exposure reaches an AUROC of 0.974 against 0.937 for FPR alone, 0.847
  for propensity alone and 0.379 for the judge's static accuracy (all differences $p < 0.001$ after Holm correction).

  and evaluation summaries of every run at \repourl.

% ============================================================================ 2 setting
\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Hacking patterns.} We study 14 families. Thirteen come from \citet{huang2025accuracy} and range from
empty symbols and gibberish to adversarial prefixes and answer explanations. The fourteenth covers reasoning
openers such as \str{Thought process:}, taken from \citet{zhao2025onetoken} (Appendix~\ref{app:families}).
Rule-based detectors assign each response to at most one family. A response belongs to a family only if the
pattern dominates it rather than merely appearing in it, so a response with a non-empty boxed answer or real
mathematical work is never a family member. The detectors agree with all 6,123
labeled answers of the hacking set and flag none of 12,500 reference solutions from MATH and DeepScaleR.

\paragraph{Judges.} Four core judges span the range of vulnerability reported by \citet{huang2025accuracy}:
R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B, exploited in their RL runs and our positive control; DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B
\citep{deepseek2025r1}; Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct \citep{yang2024qwen25math}; and xVerify-0.5B-I
\citep{chen2025xverify}, almost never fooled and our negative control. General-Verifier
\citep{ma2025generalreasoner}, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25} and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-FP8}, released in 2026.} are held out and
serve only to test transfer. Each judge uses its native prompt and decoding (Appendix~\ref{app:judges}) and sees the
same answer string in the audit and in training. The six judges we measured reach a balanced accuracy of 0.93 to 0.99 on
the policy's own answers, so honest rewards are reliable.

\paragraph{Policy, problems and gold labels.} The policy is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25}. Training
problems are integer-answer problems from DeepScaleR \citep{luo2025deepscaler} and the MATH training split
\citep{hendrycks2021math}, excluding the 471 problems behind the hacking set and any overlap with MATH-500
\citep{lightman2023verify} and the GSM8K test set \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}. A rule-based gold checker labels correctness by comparing the last \verb|\boxed{}| answer with the reference through Math-Verify \citep{huggingface2025mathverify}, and the policy never sees it. It agrees with a blind hand audit on 195 of 200 responses (97.5\%, or 99.7\% with each sampled stratum
weighted by its size). Every disagreement is a correct answer written without a box.

\paragraph{Difficulty pools.} The base policy answers each of 21,845 candidate problems eight times. The hard pool
holds the problems it solves once or twice (base accuracy 16.6\%), and the easy pool those it solves five to seven
times (76.1\%). Each pool has 2,229 training and 100 evaluation problems, because the 4,662 hard problems are subsampled at random to match the 2,329 easy ones.

% ============================================================================ 3 method
\section{Predicting exploitation before training}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_fpr_heatmap.pdf}
  \caption{Judge audit. Each cell is the share of 471 math problems on which a judge accepts a family's seeding
  string as a correct answer. Families are ordered by mean FPR over the four core judges. xVerify accepts no string on more than 0.4\% of problems, and the held-out Qwen3.6-35B-A3B accepts essentially one string.}
  \label{fig:fpr}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Judge audit.} Each family is represented by one pre-specified seeding string. It is the family's most
frequent answer in the hacking set, except that reasoning openers use \str{Thought process:} and gibberish uses fresh random
strings. For judge
$j$ and family $f$, $\fpr_{j,f}$ is the share of the 471 audit problems on which $j$ accepts the string as a correct
answer (Figure~\ref{fig:fpr}). We audit strings rather than family averages because GRPO reinforces the strings the
policy actually samples, and within a family the FPR of a string can differ from the family mean by up to 67
points. The audit takes 6,594 judge calls on short strings per judge, shared by all runs with that judge.

\paragraph{Controlled propensity.} Each starting policy is the base policy after two epochs of SFT on 2,000 of
its own finished and correct answers to problems outside both pools. A share of these answers is replaced by seeding
strings.
The 14 families were split once at random into groups of five, five and four. Seed $s$ uses rotation
$s$, in which the groups are seeded at 0.3\%, 1.2\% and 4.0\% in cyclic order. Every family thus meets every dose
across the three seeds. All 14 families are seeded into the same set so that they compete within each run,
and seeding strings make up 23.5\%, 27.2\% and 26.3\% of the 2,000 examples under rotations 0, 1 and 2. A matched 0\% control replaces nothing. We then measure the seeded propensity
$\phat_f$ by sampling the fine-tuned policy 16 times on each of 250 problems per pool, with Jeffreys smoothing.
These 4,000 samples per checkpoint and pool are about 5\% of the 76,800 responses that one GRPO run samples. They
suffice because every event in our runs had an exposure of at least 1.92 (Section~\ref{sec:results}). That needs
$\phat\,\fpr \ge 0.0075$, so a family that can win appears about 30 times or more in the sample. A family absent from all 4,000 samples has $\phat$ below
$7.5 \times 10^{-4}$ with 95\% confidence and an exposure of at most 0.19.
In every checkpoint and pool the measured propensity reproduces the order of the seeded doses, with no family of a
lower dose measured above one of a higher dose.

\paragraph{Exposure score.} The score of family $f$ for a run with judge $j$ on a pool is
\begin{equation}
  r_{j,f} = \phat_f \cdot \fpr_{j,f},
\end{equation}
and $256\,r_{j,f}$ is the expected number of rewarded responses of family $f$ per GRPO step. Propensity is measured
on the starting checkpoint and the pool of the run, so the score of a family changes with the rotation of its seed.
$\fpr_{j,f}$ depends only on the judge.

\paragraph{Outcome.} Every 10 steps the policy answers the 100 evaluation problems of its pool twice. The
exploitation rate $e_f(t)$ is the share of these 200 outputs that belong to family $f$, are wrong by gold and are
accepted by the judge. Family $f$ has an \emph{event} when $e_f(t)$ exceeds $e_f(0)$ by at least $\tau = 0.05$ at
two consecutive evaluations, and the event time is the first of them. Measuring from step 0 keeps a judge's initial
errors from counting as hacking.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_h1.pdf}
  \caption{Prediction against outcome. (a) Exposure recorded before training against the largest rise of $e_f(t)$
  within 300 steps, for all 462 patterns of the seeded runs. The dashed line marks 1.92, the lowest exposure of any event. Above it, 9 hard-pool patterns without an event lost to a higher-exposure winner of their run (triangles),
  and one was the top pattern of a run without an event (diamond). (b) Rank of the pattern exploited first in the 12
  runs with an event.}
  \label{fig:h1}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Runs.} The core design crosses the four core judges with the two pools and three seeds, for 24 runs. The held-out judges are trained on the hard pool with the three seeds, for 9 runs.
Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct is the most vulnerable judge, and the 0\% control pairs it with the hard pool and three
seeds for 3 more runs. Each run trains the policy for 300 GRPO steps of 32 prompts with 8 samples each, using TRL
\citep{vonwerra2020trl} with vLLM generation \citep{kwon2023vllm}. Training uses the token-level loss of DAPO \citep{yu2025dapo} with clipping at 0.2 and 0.28, no KL
penalty and a learning rate of $10^{-6}$. The judge is the only reward (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}).

\paragraph{Validity checks.} A judge engineered to accept every unboxed answer is exploited from step 150 after
about 300 accepted samples. The pipeline therefore detects hacking when it is possible.
The positive control trained from the unseeded policy produced 5 family responses in 76,800 training samples and no
event, as its low natural propensity predicts.

\paragraph{Analysis.} The unit is a pair of run and family. H1 compares the AUROC of exposure for events with the
AUROC of FPR alone, propensity alone and static accuracy on the 24 core runs. Static accuracy enters with a negative sign so that a less accurate judge scores higher.
Intervals come from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of whole runs within each judge. Resampling whole runs keeps together
the families that compete for the same takeover, and it pairs the three differences. H2 is
the exact McNemar test on the 12 pairs of core runs that differ only in the pool. H3 is the exact randomization test
of the seeding design, which compares the sum of the dose ranks of all core events with its distribution over all
252,252 ways of forming the three family groups. Holm correction covers these five tests. Secondary analyses model time to the first event with
cause-specific hazards, the winner with a conditional logit, and the early growth of every family
(Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Every exposure score is computed from static measurements taken before the run it
predicts.

\paragraph{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited (H1).} Fourteen events occurred in 12 of the 21 seeded
hard-pool runs and none on the easy pool. Nine came from 7 of the 24 core runs and five from 5 of the 9 held-out runs, while the 3 unseeded control runs had none.
As Figure~\ref{fig:h1}a shows, no family with an exposure below 1.92 rewarded responses per step had an event. Of
the 10 hard-pool families above that level without one, 9 lost to a family of higher exposure in the same run.
The tenth had an exposure of 2.74 and was the top family of a General-Verifier run without any event. On the core runs
exposure reaches an AUROC of 0.974 (95\% interval 0.955 to 0.995), ahead of FPR alone by 0.037 (0.020 to 0.055),
propensity alone by 0.127 and static accuracy by 0.595 (Figure~\ref{fig:auroc}, Table~\ref{tab:tests}). Without the positive control, exposure still reaches 0.979 against 0.945 for FPR. Rankings make the advantage of exposure plainer. In all 12 runs with an event, the pattern exploited first was ranked first by exposure, whereas FPR alone ranked it first in 6
runs and propensity alone in 2 (Figure~\ref{fig:h1}b). Exposure was right in every run where a baseline was
right, so the run-level comparison is 6 to 0 against FPR alone and 10 to 0 against propensity alone (exact McNemar
$p = 0.031$ and $0.002$). Averaged within these runs, the AUROC is 0.990 for exposure, 0.954 for FPR alone and
0.837 for propensity alone. The gain over FPR comes from high-FPR strings that the seeding
put at a low dose, such as \str{\{} for R1-Distill-Verifier in seed 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:takeover}).

\paragraph{Both factors are needed.} If exposure is the right quantity, the winner of a run should be predicted by
log propensity and log FPR with equal weights. In a conditional logit over the 12 runs with an event, every weight
ratio from 0.46 to 1.66 ranks every winner first. This range contains the equal weights of the product. Neither
factor suffices alone. In 5 of the 12 runs the winner was neither the family with the highest FPR nor the one with
the highest propensity. Over all seeded runs, a cause-specific Cox model gives a hazard ratio of 10.5 (5.1 to 58.8) per unit of log
exposure.
The two factors act at different moments. Under group normalization a sampled family gains probability when its
expected reward exceeds the mean reward of its group. That expected reward is its FPR, so FPR sets the direction of
early growth (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Propensity sets the base from which that growth compounds. A takeover needs
both. Every winner
had collected between 234 and 804 rewarded responses (median 586) before its event. Exposure is the initial rate at
which a family collects them, so the product decides which family gets there first within the budget. It also predicts when. Over the
14 events the event step falls with exposure (Spearman $-0.84$, against $-0.43$ for FPR and $-0.52$ for propensity),
and on log scales the slope for the 12 first events is $-1.03$, as a fixed number of rewards collected at the rate
of exposure implies.

\paragraph{Hard prompts gate exploitation (H2).} In 7 of the 12 pairs of core runs that differ only in the pool, a
family was exploited on the hard pool and none on the easy pool. The reverse never happened ($p = 0.016$, Holm
$0.031$). Easy-pool families with exposures as high as 9.4 rewarded responses per step stayed flat
(Figure~\ref{fig:h1}a). The mechanism is the group normalization of GRPO, which centers the rewards of a group of 8
and divides them by their sample standard deviation. A rewarded response in a group with acceptance rate $q$
therefore receives the advantage $\sqrt{7(1-q)/(8q)}$. In the first 10 steps the vulnerable judges accept 19\% to 23\% of
responses on hard prompts and 61\% to 67\% on easy ones. At these mean rates the advantage is 1.7 to 2.0 on hard
prompts against 0.7 to 0.8 on easy ones (Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}b). Dropping the division by the standard deviation
as Dr.~GRPO does \citep{liu2025understanding} would still leave 0.77 to 0.81 against 0.33 to 0.39. The gap does not come from problem source. On the step-0 evaluation outputs these judges
accept 17\% to 22\% on hard prompts and 58\% to 63\% on easy ones. DeepScaleR problems make up
85\% of the hard pool and 40\% of the easy pool. On these problems alone the judges accept 18\% to 22\% against
53\% to 59\%, for an advantage of 1.7 to 2.0 against 0.8 to 0.9. Nor do the easy problems make the strings less
acceptable. In the first 10 training steps the judges accepted 14.7\% of family responses on each pool. After the
runs we also scored each family by its expected advantage $\phat\,(\fpr - q_0)/\sqrt{q_0(1-q_0)}$, where $q_0$ is
the judge's acceptance of the starting policy's 200 step-0 evaluation outputs. This score unifies H1 and H2. It
raises the AUROC on the core runs from 0.974 to 0.993 and still ranks all 12 winners first (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Seeding dose (H3).} Events rise with the seeded dose, from none in the 42 pairs of run and family of the
unseeded control and none of the 112 core pairs at 0.3\% to 2 of 112 at 1.2\% and 7 of 112 at 4.0\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}c). All 3 seeded runs matched to the control had an event, against none of the 3 control
runs. The dose ranks of the core events sum to 25, the second largest value the design allows. Its two-sided $p$ is 0.056 and its one-sided $p$ 0.033, while the largest possible sum would give 0.017 (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Held-out judges.} Exposure transfers to the three held-out judges. The level of 1.92 set by
the core events classifies 8 of the 9 held-out runs. The one exception is the General-Verifier run at 2.74, and none
of the 119 held-out families below the level had an event. In all 5 held-out runs with an event
the pattern exploited first was ranked first by exposure, against 3 runs for FPR alone. Exposure reaches an AUROC of
0.995 (0.986 to 1.000). These judges accept at most three strings at 10\% or more, against up to eight among the
core judges. A pooled AUROC is therefore close to its ceiling for any score that knows which strings a judge accepts, and FPR alone already reaches 0.979. The ranking of the winner separates the two, and exposure gains exactly where the first choice of
FPR was seeded at a low dose. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B accepts \str{\{} on 84.7\% of problems and
essentially no other string. Before its runs we predicted that the seed in which \str{\{} sits at 0.3\% would have no
event, and it had none.

\paragraph{Robustness.} The lead of exposure over FPR alone stays between 0.037 and 0.041 for thresholds $\tau$ from
0.025 to 0.10 with one or two consecutive evaluations. It also holds when propensity is measured as the total likelihood of the seeding strings and when FPR is averaged over each family's strings (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). Without any threshold, the largest rise of $e_f$ correlates 0.555 with exposure and 0.467 with FPR, a lead of 0.088 (0.033 to 0.141). On the hard pool alone
exposure leads 0.984 to 0.950. Every event happened on this pool, and the flat high-exposure families of the easy
pool make the pooled test harder. Replacing the measured propensity by the injected dose gives an AUROC of 0.971 and again ranks all 12 winners first, so the audit recovers the dose without being told it. Within a dose it still orders families by how well their string was learned, which is why propensity alone reaches 0.847 and the dose alone 0.766. Outside a
seeded experiment only the sampled propensity is available.

% ============================================================================ 6 related work
\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reward hacking.} Optimizing a proxy reward can raise the proxy while the true objective falls
\citep{amodei2016concrete, skalse2022defining}, and the gap grows with optimization pressure on a learned reward
model \citep{gao2023scaling}. RL with verifiable rewards mostly reweights outputs that the base model already samples
\citep{yue2025incentivize}, which makes the starting propensity of a loophole a natural factor. We study reward
hacking of LLM judges used as verifiers in RL, and ask which of a judge's flaws a policy will actually reach.

\paragraph{Robustness of LLM judges and verifiers.} LLM judges are biased and manipulable
\citep{zheng2023judging}. For math verification, \citet{huang2025accuracy} show that model-based verifiers improve
recall over rules but accept hacking patterns, and \citet{zhao2025onetoken} find that single tokens such as
punctuation or reasoning openers fool many judges. Discriminative verifiers such as xVerify \citep{chen2025xverify}
are far more robust, both to these patterns and as the reward in RL \citep{huang2025accuracy}. These works measure
what a judge accepts, while we ask which of those flaws training finds and show that static accuracy and FPR alone
are weaker predictors than exposure.

\paragraph{When reward hacking emerges.} CHERRL injects known biases into rubric judges and studies their
discoverability and exploitability during training \citep{wang2026rubric}. Countdown-Code shows that 1\% of hacking
trajectories in distillation data makes hacking resurface in RL \citep{khalifa2026countdown}. In coding RL with designed-in loopholes, CATCH shows
that a model's initial tendency to hack and the difficulty of earning reward
both shape when hacking emerges \citep{wang2026catch}. Our H2 and H3 are their counterparts for real judges. On the
verifier side, fuzzing finds inputs that fool a verifier before training \citep{ray2026fuzzing}, and injected
reward noise measures how much verifier error RL tolerates \citep{plesner2026imperfect}. Neither considers how often the policy
produces each flaw. On the policy side, benchmarks record exploits by agents
\citep{thaman2026benchmark} and in rubric RL \citep{mahmoud2026rubric} once they occur, and fine-tuning has stood in
for RL to anticipate hacking \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}. None of these ranks competing loopholes before training. We differ in measuring strictly before
RL, in targeting the natural flaws of real judges, in predicting which of many competing loopholes wins, and in
testing on held-out judges with propensity under experimental control.

% ============================================================================ 7 conclusion
\section{Conclusion}

In GRPO training, which loophole of an LLM judge a policy exploits is predictable before training. It is the
pattern with the highest exposure, the product of how often the policy produces it and how often the judge accepts
it. Difficulty decides whether any pattern is reached at all. Neither a judge's static accuracy nor its FPR alone
tells which flaw training will find. The audit needs the judge's verdicts on 14 strings and 4,000 samples
of the starting policy, about 5\% of the samples of one training run. It ranked the winner first in all 12 runs with a takeover, and both run-level predictions written
down before their runs held. No xVerify run had an event, and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B had none in the seed where its one
strongly accepted string was seeded at 0.3\%.

% ============================================================================ limitations (required, unnumbered)
\section*{Limitations}

Our evidence comes from one 1.5B policy trained with GRPO for 300 steps on integer-answer math, with 14 known pattern
families and judges that fit beside training on one GPU. Larger policies may find patterns outside the families we detect.
At this scale natural propensities keep every pattern out of reach, so propensity was raised by controlled seeding.
About a quarter of the SFT examples were replaced by seeding strings across the 14 families. This tests the account
with propensity under control, but it does not model natural fine-tuning data or the spontaneous emergence of these
patterns. Exploitation is
winner-take-all within a run. Although 12 of the 21 seeded hard-pool runs had an event, the first winner crowds out the other
families. A run therefore yields one or two events (14 in all), and per-judge intervals are wide.
Transfer is tested on three held-out judges.
The dose test (H3) is bounded by the resolution of a design with three family groups (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). The hard and easy pools
differ in problem source as well as difficulty. The acceptance gap holds within DeepScaleR problems, but events could
not be compared within one source. These limits bound how far the claim generalizes. Within the
setting, the seeded dose was assigned at random through the Latin square and every exposure score was computed before its
run, which rules out post-hoc fitting.

\section*{Ethics statement}

The hacking patterns studied here were published before this work \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}. Our
results show which of them a policy trained against the judge exploits. This helps practitioners screen judges before RL and helps
evaluators who rely on LLM judges, but it could also help someone game a judge-based evaluation. We report every
pattern and judge so that these weaknesses are known and testable. All data and models are used for research under
their licenses. xVerify-0.5B-I is licensed for non-commercial use and was used for inference only. The experiments
used about 212 GPU hours.

\section{Statistical details and sensitivity analyses}
\label{app:stats}

\paragraph{H1.} AUROC counts ties as one half. Each of 10,000 bootstrap replicates resamples whole runs with
replacement within each judge. Replicates without an event are dropped, which happened once in 10,000 on the core
runs. Differences to each baseline use the same replicates and units, with the two-sided $p$-value
$2\min(P(d \le 0), P(d \ge 0))$. A $p$ of 0 enters the Holm correction as $10^{-4}$. On the held-out runs exposure leads FPR alone by 0.017 (0.000 to
0.035, $p = 0.066$).

\paragraph{Run-level checks.} Over the 12 runs with an event, the mean reciprocal rank of the first winner is 1.00
for exposure, 0.71 for FPR alone and 0.52 for propensity alone. The AUROC within each of these runs counts all its events as
positives. Exposure has a higher one than FPR alone in 6 runs and a lower one in none (sign test $p = 0.031$), and a
higher one than propensity alone in 11 runs and a lower one in none ($p = 0.001$). Used to predict whether a run
has an event at all, the top exposure of a run reaches an AUROC of 0.865 over the 33 seeded runs and 0.981 over the
21 seeded hard-pool runs, against 0.815 and 0.944 for the top FPR. On the hard pool 12 of the 13 runs whose top
family reaches 1.92 had an event, and none of the 8 below it. The lowest exposure of a core event is 1.92 and that of
a held-out event 2.09.

\paragraph{Second events and event time.} Two runs had a second event. Its family ranks third
(Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct, seed 1) and second (R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B, seed 2) by exposure among the 13 families
left after the winner. Over the 14 events the event step has a Spearman correlation of $-0.84$ with exposure
(permutation $p = 0.0003$), $-0.43$ with FPR and $-0.52$ with propensity. The least-squares slope of log step on log
exposure is $-0.88$ over all events and $-1.03$ over the 12 first events. The kinetic form
$\log(1/\phat)/(\fpr - q_0)$ estimates the time a family needs to grow from its starting share at the rate set by its
excess acceptance. It correlates 0.73 with the event step.

\paragraph{H2 and H3.} H2 is the exact McNemar test on the 12 pairs of core runs that share judge, checkpoint and seed.
The outcome of a run is whether some family has an event. The advantages of Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}b are
plug-in values at the mean acceptance rate, since the training logs keep only flagged responses. In the first 10
training steps the core judges accepted 14.7\% of family responses on both pools. This covers the 56 pairs of judge
and family with at least 50 responses in each pool. The hard pool is higher in 23 of them and the easy pool in 22,
and both stay close to the audit FPR. For H3 the statistic is the sum over all core events of
the dose rank (1 to 3) that the event's family received. Its null distribution is taken over all 252,252 ways of splitting
the 14 families into groups of five, five and four with outcomes held fixed. This test is exact under the null
hypothesis that the dose changes no outcome, including through competition within a run. With all events on four
families, the largest statistic the design allows is 26, which would give a two-sided $p$ of 0.017. The observed
statistic of 25 is the second largest (two-sided $p = 0.056$, one-sided 0.033).

\paragraph{Difficulty-aware score.} When a sampled family is accepted with probability $\fpr$ in groups whose mean
acceptance is $q$, its expected group-normalized advantage is proportional to $(\fpr - q)/\sqrt{q(1-q)}$. Here $q_0$ is
the judge's acceptance of the 200 step-0 evaluation outputs of a run, which the starting policy produces before any
update. Each family is scored by $\phat\,(\fpr - q_0)/\sqrt{q_0(1-q_0)}$. This score was defined after
the runs. Its AUROC is 0.993 on the core runs, 0.988 on the core hard pool and 0.998 on the held-out runs, against
0.974, 0.984 and 0.995 for exposure. On the core runs it leads exposure by 0.019 (0.004 to 0.037, $p = 0.008$) and
FPR alone by 0.056 (0.030 to 0.082), and it ranks all 12 winners first. It gains on the easy pool, where most
families of high exposure are accepted about as often as an average response and so gain little advantage.

\paragraph{Secondary analyses.} Log models use the Jeffreys-smoothed FPR $(k + 0.5)/(n + 1)$, since many FPRs are 0.
Censoring each family at the first event of another family in its run gives cause-specific hazards. A Cox model was fitted only where there are at least 10 events per covariate, which holds for the 12 first events of all 33 seeded runs and not for the 7 of the core runs alone. The winner conditional logit separates the data for the full and the product model, so
their likelihoods are suprema. Its likelihood-ratio tests of FPR alone ($p = 1.1 \times 10^{-6}$) and propensity
alone ($p = 1.4 \times 10^{-11}$) therefore rest on asymptotics that separation strains, and the exact McNemar tests
of Section~\ref{sec:results} carry the run-level comparison. Early growth is the change in a family's log share of the training responses from steps 0 to 9 to the 10
steps ending 10 steps before the run's first event, or to steps 40 to 49 in a run without one. Each share adds 0.5
to the family's count before the log is taken. Its Spearman correlation is 0.28 with FPR, $-0.04$ with exposure and $-0.33$ with propensity, since seeded strings decay unless the judge accepts them.

\paragraph{Sensitivity analyses.} Each variant changes one part of the primary test and repeats it on the core runs
(Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). The event definition varies $\tau$ over half, once and twice the primary value, each with
one or two consecutive evaluations above the step-0 value. The looser definitions add single-evaluation rises of 5 to
13 outputs in 200. Propensity is replaced by the total log-likelihood of the family's canonical strings under the
fine-tuned policy, by their per-token log-likelihood or by the seeded dose, and the likelihoods are combined with FPR
in log space. FPR is replaced by the stage-1 mean over each family's strings in the hacking set. This variant leaves
out Qwen3.6, which was not audited in stage 1. The last variant keeps only the hard pool. The lead of exposure over FPR alone stays between 0.033 and
0.041 except for per-token likelihood, which favors short repetitive strings and is not a frequency.
