% ============================================================================ abstract
\begin{abstract}
Which loophole of an LLM judge will a policy exploit when the judge serves as its reward? LLM judges accept
degenerate answers such as a lone \str{\{} or \str{Thought process:}, but static probes list such flaws without
saying which ones training will find. In GRPO, the answer can be read off before training from each pattern's
exposure, the product of the starting policy's propensity to produce it and the judge's false-positive rate (FPR).
To test exposure causally, we seed the patterns into fine-tuning at three doses in a Latin square. Across 36 GRPO runs, the top-exposure
pattern was exploited first in all 12 runs with a takeover, against 6 for FPR alone. Exposure reached an AUROC of
0.974 on four core judges against 0.937 for FPR alone, and 0.995 on three held-out judges. The takeover step fell roughly as
one over exposure. In four further runs with one pattern masked by rule, exposure predicted the winner and its takeover window in three, against none for
FPR alone. Exploitation occurred only on hard prompts, where rewards are scarce and group normalization gives an
accepted exploit a large advantage. Auditing exposure before RL thus identifies which loophole to patch first.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\begin{figure*}[t]

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become a standard way to train reasoning in language models
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath, deepseek2025r1}. When an answer cannot be checked by a rule, an LLM is often asked to
act as the verifier, comparing the policy's answer with a reference \citep{chen2025xverify, ma2025generalreasoner}.
Such judges can grade a lone \str{\{}, a bare claim that the answer is correct, or
the words \str{Thought process:} as correct \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}, and a policy trained
against such a judge can learn to emit these strings instead of solving the problem
\citep{huang2025accuracy}.

Knowing that a judge has a blind spot does not tell us whether training will find it. \citet{huang2025accuracy}
observe that DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B failed static probes at high rates yet showed no reward hacking in their
RL runs, while a more accurate verifier fine-tuned from it was exploited. They suggest that their policies were too weak to exploit the untrained judge, but static
probes measure what a judge accepts, not how often the policy samples each flaw. Studies of when hacking begins inject known biases into rubric judges \citep{wang2026rubric}, build loopholes into coding environments
\citep{khalifa2026countdown, wang2026catch}, or replace RL with fine-tuning \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}.
Prior work has not tested whether auditing the judge and the policy before RL can predict which of a real judge's
existing flaws a policy will exploit and whether such predictions hold on held-out judges.

We start from a simple account of how a policy finds a loophole under GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. A pattern
can be reinforced only when the policy samples it and the judge rewards it. With $N$ responses per training step,
a pattern produced at rate $\phat$ and accepted with probability $\fpr$ earns on average $N\,\phat\,\fpr$ rewarded
responses per step, its \emph{exposure}. We hypothesize that the pattern with the highest exposure is the one
exploited, and that neither factor alone predicts as well (H1). Group normalization adds difficulty as a second
factor, because an accepted exploit is often the only rewarded response in its group when correct answers are rare
and then receives a large advantage. Hard prompts should therefore raise the risk (H2). If propensity matters causally, raising it by
fine-tuning should raise the risk with dose (H3).

The account already explains why a small policy left to itself finds no loophole. Only 19 of the 174,760
responses that our base policy gave to the 21,845 candidate problems of Section~\ref{sec:setting} fall in any of
14 hacking-pattern families, a rate of about $10^{-4}$. Even a judge that accepted all of them would start a run at an exposure below 0.03 rewarded responses per step, about 70 times below
the lowest exposure of any pattern exploited in our runs. In the unseeded runs no pattern is exploited within 300
steps. Outside a controlled setting the two factors can also move together, for example when judges accept a format the
policy favors. A correlation with exposure would then not show which factor drives exploitation. To separate the two, we set propensity by design. About 1\% of hacking trajectories in
fine-tuning data makes hacking resurface in RL \citep{khalifa2026countdown}, so we seed each pattern into a short
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage on the policy's own correct answers at 0.3\%, 1.2\% or 4.0\% of the examples. The doses rotate over three seeds in a Latin square so that every
pattern meets every dose at random and independently of its FPR
(Figure~\ref{fig:takeover}). All 14 patterns are seeded into the same starting policy and compete within each run. In total, seeding strings make up 23.5\% to 27.2\% of the SFT examples. We
measure each judge's FPR on each pattern and the seeded propensity, record every score, and only then train
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_overview.pdf}
  \caption{Overview of the method. (1) Before training, each judge is audited on one seeding string per family for its FPR, and each seeded starting policy is sampled for its propensity. (2) Their product is the exposure, which is recorded as the
  prediction. Group normalization gives an accepted exploit a large advantage on hard prompts. (3) In GRPO the
  judge is the only reward, while a monitor that the policy never sees measures the exploitation rate of every
  family.}
  \label{fig:overview}
\end{figure*}

We train Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with GRPO against four core and three held-out judges in 40 runs, 4 of which mask one
pattern by rule. We find:

\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited.} In all 12 unmasked runs with an event, the pattern exploited first
  was the one ranked first by exposure, against 6 runs for FPR alone. Over the 336 pooled pairs of run and pattern of the 24 core runs, exposure reaches an AUROC of 0.974 against 0.937 for FPR alone, 0.847
  for propensity alone and 0.379 for the judge's static accuracy (all differences $p < 0.001$ after Holm correction).
  On the 9 held-out runs it reaches 0.995. With one pattern masked by
  rule, exposure named 3 of 4 winners and FPR alone none, so it ranked the winner first in 15 of all 16
  runs with an event.
\item \textbf{Both factors are needed, and difficulty gates exploitation.} In 5 of the 12 unmasked runs the
  winner was neither the pattern with the highest FPR nor the one with the highest propensity. The takeover step also falls
  roughly as one over exposure. Takeovers occur only on hard prompts, where an accepted exploit receives about 2.6 times the group-normalized advantage it gets on easy
  prompts at the mean acceptance rate.
\item \textbf{An open audit.} We release the FPR of seven open judges on 14 patterns, the code, and the training
  and evaluation summaries of every run at \repourl.
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================================ 2 setting
\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Hacking patterns.} We study 14 families. Thirteen come from \citet{huang2025accuracy} and range from
empty symbols and gibberish to adversarial prefixes and answer explanations. The fourteenth comes from \citet{zhao2025onetoken} and covers reasoning openers such as \str{Thought process:}
(Appendix~\ref{app:families}).
Rule-based detectors assign each response to at most one family. A response belongs to a family only if the
pattern dominates it, so a response with a non-empty boxed answer or real
mathematical work is never a family member. The detectors agree with all 6,123
labeled answers of the hacking set and flag none of 12,500 reference solutions from MATH and DeepScaleR.

\paragraph{Judges.} Four core judges span the range of vulnerability reported by \citet{huang2025accuracy}:
R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B, exploited in their RL runs and used as our positive control; DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B
\citep{deepseek2025r1}; Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct \citep{yang2024qwen25math}; and xVerify-0.5B-I
\citep{chen2025xverify}, almost never fooled and used as our negative control. General-Verifier
\citep{ma2025generalreasoner}, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25} and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-FP8}, released in 2026.} are held out and
serve only to test transfer. Each judge uses its native prompt and decoding (Appendix~\ref{app:judges}) and sees the
same answer string in the audit and in training. The six judges we measured reach a balanced accuracy of 0.93 to 0.99 on
the policy's own answers, so honest rewards are reliable.

\paragraph{Policy, problems and gold labels.} The policy is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25}. Training
problems are integer-answer problems from DeepScaleR \citep{luo2025deepscaler} and the MATH training split
\citep{hendrycks2021math}, excluding the 471 problems behind the hacking set and any overlap with MATH-500
\citep{lightman2023verify} and the GSM8K test set \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}. The base policy answers each of these 21,845
candidate problems eight times. The hard pool
holds the problems it solves once or twice (base accuracy 16.6\%), and the easy pool those it solves five to seven
times (76.1\%). Each pool has 2,229 training and 100 evaluation problems, because the 4,662 hard problems are subsampled at random to match the 2,329 easy ones. A rule-based gold checker labels correctness by comparing the last \verb|\boxed{}| answer with the reference through Math-Verify \citep{huggingface2025mathverify}, and the policy never sees it. The checker agrees with a blind hand audit on 195 of 200 responses (97.5\%, or 99.7\% with each sampled stratum
weighted by its size). Every disagreement is a correct answer written without a box.

% ============================================================================ 3 method
\section{Predicting exploitation before training}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_fpr_heatmap.pdf}
  \caption{Judge audit. Each cell is the share of 471 math problems on which a judge accepts a family's seeding
  string as a correct answer. Families are ordered by mean FPR over the four core judges. xVerify accepts no string on more than 0.4\% of problems, and the held-out Qwen3.6-35B-A3B accepts essentially one string.}
  \label{fig:fpr}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Judge audit.} The seeding string of each family is fixed in advance as its
most frequent answer in the hacking set, except that reasoning openers use \str{Thought process:} and gibberish uses fresh random
strings. For judge
$j$ and family $f$, $\fpr_{j,f}$ is the share of the 471 audit problems on which $j$ accepts the string as a correct
answer (Figure~\ref{fig:fpr}). We audit strings rather than family averages because GRPO reinforces the strings the
policy actually samples, and within a family the FPR of a string can differ from the family mean by up to 67
points. The audit takes 6,594 judge calls on short strings per judge, and all runs with that judge share it.

\paragraph{Controlled propensity.} Each starting policy is the base policy after two epochs of SFT on 2,000 of
its own finished and correct answers to problems outside both pools. A share of these answers is replaced by seeding
strings.
The 14 families were split once at random into groups of five, five and four. Seed $s$ uses rotation
$s$, in which the groups are seeded at 0.3\%, 1.2\% and 4.0\% in cyclic order. Every family thus meets every dose
across the three seeds. All 14 families are seeded into the same set so that they compete within each run,
and seeding strings make up 23.5\%, 27.2\% and 26.3\% of the 2,000 examples under rotations 0, 1 and 2. A matched 0\% control uses the same set with no example replaced. We then measure the seeded propensity
$\phat_f$ by sampling the fine-tuned policy 16 times on each of 250 problems per pool, with Jeffreys smoothing.
The 4,000 samples per checkpoint and pool are about 5\% of the 76,800 responses sampled in one GRPO run and are
enough to find every family that can win. Every event in our runs had an exposure of at least 1.92
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), which requires $\phat\,\fpr \ge 0.0075$ and hence about 30 occurrences or more in
4,000 samples. A family absent from all 4,000 samples has $\phat$ below
$7.5 \times 10^{-4}$ with 95\% confidence and an exposure of at most 0.19.
In every checkpoint and pool the measured propensity reproduces the order of the seeded doses, with no family of a lower dose ranked above one of a higher dose.

\paragraph{Exposure score.} The score of family $f$ for a run with judge $j$ on a pool is
\begin{equation}
  r_{j,f} = \phat_f \cdot \fpr_{j,f},
\end{equation}
and $256\,r_{j,f}$ is the expected number of rewarded responses of family $f$ per GRPO step. Propensity is measured
on the starting checkpoint and the pool of the run, so the score of a family changes with the rotation of its seed,
while $\fpr_{j,f}$ depends only on the judge.

  \label{fig:h1}

\paragraph{Outcome.} Every 10 steps the policy answers the 100 evaluation problems of its pool twice. The
exploitation rate $e_f(t)$ is the share of these 200 outputs that belong to family $f$, are wrong by gold and are
accepted by the judge. Family $f$ has an \emph{event} when $e_f(t)$ exceeds $e_f(0)$ by at least $\tau = 0.05$ at
two consecutive evaluations, and the event time is the first of them. Measuring from step 0 keeps a judge's initial
errors from counting as hacking.

\paragraph{Runs.} The core design crosses the four core judges with the two pools and three seeds, for 24 runs. The held-out judges add 9 runs on the hard pool with the three seeds.
Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct is the most vulnerable judge, and the 0\% control pairs it with the hard pool and three
seeds for 3 more runs. Each run trains the policy for 300 GRPO steps of 32 prompts with 8 samples each, using TRL
\citep{vonwerra2020trl} with vLLM generation \citep{kwon2023vllm}. Training uses the token-level loss of DAPO \citep{yu2025dapo} with clipping at 0.2 and 0.28, no KL
penalty and a learning rate of $10^{-6}$. The judge is the only reward (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}). A judge engineered to accept every unboxed answer is
exploited from step 150 after about 300 accepted samples, so the pipeline detects hacking when it is possible. The
positive control trained from the unseeded policy produced 5 family responses in 76,800 training samples and no
event, as its low natural propensity predicts. We also repeat four core hard-pool runs with one pattern rejected by rule (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Analysis.} The unit is a pair of run and family. H1 compares the AUROC of exposure for events with the
AUROC of FPR alone, propensity alone and static accuracy on the 24 core runs. Static accuracy enters with a negative sign so that a less accurate judge scores higher.
Intervals come from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of whole runs within each judge. Resampling whole runs keeps together
the families that compete for the same takeover and pairs the differences between exposure and each baseline. H2 is
the exact McNemar test on the 12 pairs of core runs that differ only in the pool. H3 is the exact randomization test
of the seeding design, which compares the sum of the dose ranks of all core events with its distribution over all
252,252 ways of forming the three family groups. Holm correction covers these five tests. Secondary analyses model time to the first event with cause-specific hazards and the winner with a conditional
logit, and they trace the early growth of every family (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Every exposure score uses only measurements taken before its run.

\paragraph{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited (H1).} As Figures~\ref{fig:h1}b and~\ref{fig:auroc} show, exposure
ranks the first winner first in all 12 unmasked runs with an event and reaches an AUROC of 0.974 (95\% interval
0.955 to 0.995) on the core runs. FPR alone names 6 of these winners and reaches 0.937, while propensity alone
names 2 and reaches 0.847. Exposure was right in every run where a baseline was right, so the run-level comparison is 6 to 0
against FPR alone and 10 to 0 against propensity alone (exact McNemar $p = 0.031$ and $0.002$). On the core runs
exposure leads FPR alone by 0.037 (0.020 to 0.055), propensity alone by 0.127 and static accuracy by 0.595
(Table~\ref{tab:tests}). The 14 events occurred in 12 of the 21
seeded hard-pool runs and none on the easy pool (Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}a). Nine came from 7 of the 24 core runs and five from 5 of the 9
held-out runs, while the 3 unseeded control runs had none. As Figure~\ref{fig:h1}a shows, no family with an exposure
below 1.92 rewarded responses per step had an event. Of the 10 hard-pool families above that level without one, 9
lost to a family of higher exposure in the same run. The tenth had an exposure of 2.74 and was the top family of a
General-Verifier run without any event. Averaged within the 12 runs with an event, the AUROC is 0.990 for exposure,
0.954 for FPR alone and 0.837 for propensity alone. The gain over FPR comes from high-FPR strings that the seeding
put at a low dose, such as \str{\{} for R1-Distill-Verifier in seed 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:takeover}).

\paragraph{Both factors are needed.} In 5 of the 12 unmasked runs the winner had
neither the highest FPR nor the highest propensity of its run, so only their product identifies it. In a conditional
logit over these runs, every weight ratio of log propensity to log FPR from 0.46 to 1.66 ranks every winner first,
including the equal weights of the product. Over all seeded runs, a cause-specific Cox model gives a hazard ratio of 10.5 (5.1 to 58.8) per unit of log
exposure.
Under group normalization a sampled family gains probability when its
expected reward exceeds the mean reward of its group. FPR is that expected reward and so sets the direction of early
growth (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}), while propensity sets the base from which the growth compounds. Every winner
had collected between 234 and 804 rewarded responses (median 586) before its event. Exposure is the initial rate at
which a family collects them, so the product decides which family gets there first within the budget. The event step also falls with exposure over the 14
events (Figure~\ref{fig:h1}c, Spearman $-0.84$, against $-0.43$ for FPR and $-0.52$ for propensity),
and on log scales the slope for the 12 first events is $-1.03$. A fixed number of rewards collected
at the rate of exposure implies a slope of $-1$.

\paragraph{Hard prompts gate exploitation (H2).} In 7 of the 12 pairs of core runs that differ only in the pool, a
family was exploited on the hard pool and none on the easy pool. The reverse never happened ($p = 0.016$, Holm
$0.031$). Easy-pool families with exposures as high as 9.4 rewarded responses per step stayed flat
(Figure~\ref{fig:h1}a). The mechanism is the group normalization of GRPO, which centers the rewards of a group of 8
and divides them by their sample standard deviation. A rewarded response in a group with acceptance rate $q$
therefore receives the advantage $\sqrt{7(1-q)/(8q)}$. In the first 10 steps the vulnerable judges accept 19\% to 23\% of
responses on hard prompts and 61\% to 67\% on easy ones. At these mean rates the advantage is 1.7 to 2.0 on hard
prompts against 0.7 to 0.8 on easy ones (Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}b). Dropping the division by the standard deviation
as Dr.~GRPO does \citep{liu2025understanding} would still leave 0.77 to 0.81 against 0.33 to 0.39. On the step-0 evaluation outputs these judges accept 17\% to 22\% on hard
prompts and 58\% to 63\% on easy ones. DeepScaleR problems make up 85\% of the hard pool and 40\% of the easy pool. On these problems alone the judges still accept 18\% to 22\% against 53\% to 59\%, for an advantage of 1.7 to 2.0
against 0.8 to 0.9. In the first 10 training steps the judges accepted 14.7\% of family responses on each pool, so
the strings themselves are no less acceptable on easy problems. The group-normalized advantage that a family
collects per sampled response joins H1 and H2 in the score $\phat\,(\fpr - q_0)/\sqrt{q_0(1-q_0)}$, where $q_0$ is the judge's acceptance of
the starting policy's outputs before any update. Scoring families by it raises the AUROC on the core runs
from 0.974 to 0.993 (Figure~\ref{fig:auroc}) and still ranks all 12 winners first. Like exposure, the score uses only measurements taken before training and has
no fitted parameter (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Seeding dose (H3).} Events rise with the seeded dose, from none in the 42 pairs of run and family of the
unseeded control and none of the 112 core pairs at 0.3\% to 2 of 112 at 1.2\% and 7 of 112 at 4.0\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}c). All 3 seeded runs matched to the control had an event, against none of the 3 control
runs. The dose ranks of the core events sum to 25, the second largest value the design allows. The two-sided $p$ of this sum is 0.056 and the one-sided $p$ 0.033, while the largest possible sum would give 0.017 (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Held-out judges.} Exposure transfers to the three held-out judges. The level of 1.92 set by
the core events correctly predicts whether 8 of the 9 held-out runs have an event. The one exception is the General-Verifier run at 2.74, and none
of the 119 held-out families below the level had an event. In all 5 held-out runs with an event
the pattern exploited first was ranked first by exposure, against 3 runs for FPR alone. Exposure reaches an AUROC of
0.995 (0.986 to 1.000). These judges accept at most three strings at 10\% or more, against up to eight among the
core judges. A pooled AUROC is therefore close to its ceiling for any score that includes the FPR of each string, and FPR alone already reaches 0.979. The ranking of the winner separates the two, and exposure gains exactly where the first choice of
FPR was seeded at a low dose. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B accepts \str{\{} on 84.7\% of problems and
essentially no other string. Before its runs we predicted that the seed in which \str{\{} sits at 0.3\% would have no
event, and it had none.

\paragraph{Masking intervention.} We chose four core runs in which exposure and FPR alone name different winners
once one pattern is masked, and we recorded the predictions before training (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Each run was then repeated
with that pattern rejected by rule in training and evaluation. Masking the pattern that FPR alone ranks first left the winner unchanged in both such runs, and masking the main-run winner of R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B passed
the run to the runner-up by exposure. These three takeovers came at steps
60, 80 and 50 against predicted 55, 61 and 66, each inside its 90\% band. The
fourth run masked the winner of Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct and was taken over by adversarial prefixes, third by exposure among the
unmasked patterns. The predicted system-prompt mimicry stayed flat as in the unmasked run, because its 30\% acceptance
barely exceeds the 26\% group mean. The gap $\fpr - q$ is therefore close to zero, and so is its expected advantage. The expected-advantage score ranks the actual winner second among the unmasked patterns. FPR alone named none of the four winners. Over all 16 runs with an event, exposure ranked the winner first in 15, against 6 for FPR alone and 4 for propensity alone.

\paragraph{Robustness.} Across event thresholds $\tau$ from 0.025 to 0.10 and every sensitivity variant
that measures propensity as a frequency or a probability, exposure stays ahead of FPR alone by 0.033 to 0.041
(Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}).  Without any threshold, the largest rise of $e_f$ correlates 0.555 with exposure and 0.467 with FPR, a lead of 0.088 (0.033 to 0.141). On the hard pool alone
exposure leads 0.984 to 0.950. Every event happened on this pool, and the flat high-exposure families of the easy
pool make the pooled test harder. Replacing the measured propensity by the injected dose gives an AUROC of 0.971 and again ranks all 12 winners first, so propensity measured by sampling captures the injected dose. Within a dose it still orders families by how well their string was learned, which is why propensity alone reaches 0.847 and the dose alone 0.766. 

% ============================================================================ 6 related work
\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reward hacking.} Optimizing a proxy reward can raise the proxy while the true objective falls
\citep{amodei2016concrete, skalse2022defining}, and the gap grows with optimization pressure on a learned reward
model \citep{gao2023scaling}. RL with verifiable rewards mostly reweights outputs that the base model already samples
\citep{yue2025incentivize}, which makes the starting propensity of a loophole a natural factor. We study reward
hacking of LLM judges used as verifiers in RL, and ask which of a judge's flaws a policy will actually reach.

\paragraph{Robustness of LLM judges and verifiers.} LLM judges are biased and manipulable
\citep{zheng2023judging}. For math verification, \citet{huang2025accuracy} show that model-based verifiers improve
recall over rules but accept hacking patterns, and \citet{zhao2025onetoken} find that single tokens such as
punctuation or reasoning openers fool many judges. Discriminative verifiers such as xVerify \citep{chen2025xverify}
are far more robust, both to these patterns and as the reward in RL \citep{huang2025accuracy}. These works measure
what a judge accepts, while we ask which of those flaws training finds and show that static accuracy and FPR alone
are weaker predictors than exposure.

\paragraph{When reward hacking emerges.} CHERRL injects known biases into rubric judges and studies their
discoverability and exploitability during training \citep{wang2026rubric}. Countdown-Code shows that 1\% of hacking
trajectories in distillation data makes hacking resurface in RL \citep{khalifa2026countdown}. In coding RL with designed-in loopholes, CATCH shows
that a model's initial tendency to hack and the difficulty of earning reward
both shape when hacking emerges \citep{wang2026catch}. Our H2 and H3 are their counterparts for real judges. Fuzzing
\citep{ray2026fuzzing} and injected reward noise \citep{plesner2026imperfect} probe the verifier before or during
training without asking how often the policy produces each flaw. Benchmarks record exploits by agents
\citep{thaman2026benchmark} and in rubric RL \citep{mahmoud2026rubric} after they occur, and fine-tuning has stood
in for RL to anticipate hacking \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}. Our audit measures the natural flaws of real judges
before any RL step and predicts which of many competing loopholes wins. With propensity set by the seeding design and
three judges held out, the prediction is tested both causally and out of sample.

% ============================================================================ 7 conclusion
\section{Conclusion}

In GRPO training, the loophole of an LLM judge that a policy exploits is set before training by how often the
policy produces each pattern and how often the judge accepts it. A pattern takes over after collecting a few hundred
rewarded responses, and exposure is the rate at which it starts collecting them. The same product therefore decides
which pattern gets there first and how soon, with the takeover step falling roughly as one over exposure. Group
normalization gates this race by difficulty, since on hard prompts an accepted exploit is often the only reward in
its group and receives about 2.6 times the advantage it gets on easy prompts, where no pattern was exploited. Neither
a judge's static accuracy nor its FPR alone tells which flaw training will find. Measuring exposure takes the judge's
verdicts on 14 strings and 4,000 samples of the starting policy, about 5\% of the samples of one training run. This audit ranked the winner first in all 12 unmasked runs with a takeover. As the audit predicted, no xVerify run had an event,
and the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B seed whose one strongly accepted string was seeded at 0.3\% had none either. For rule-level
patches, masking the top-FPR pattern left the winner in place, while masking the top-exposure pattern passed the takeover
to another pattern. Exposure named the winner and the window of its takeover in 3 of 4 masked runs, against none
for FPR alone.

% ============================================================================ limitations (required, unnumbered)
\section*{Limitations}

Our evidence comes from one 1.5B policy trained with GRPO for 300 steps on integer-answer math, with 14 known pattern
families and judges that fit beside training on one GPU. Larger policies may find patterns outside the families we detect.
At this scale natural propensities keep every pattern out of reach, so propensity was raised by controlled seeding.
About a quarter of the SFT examples were replaced by seeding strings across the 14 families. Seeding tests the account with propensity under control, but it does not model natural fine-tuning data or the spontaneous emergence of these
patterns. Exploitation is
winner-take-all within a run. Although 12 of the 21 seeded hard-pool runs had an event, the first winner crowds out the other
families. A run therefore yields one or two events (14 in all), and per-judge intervals are wide.
Transfer is tested on three held-out judges, and the masking intervention covers four runs.
The dose test (H3) is bounded by the resolution of a design with three family groups (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). The hard and easy pools
differ in problem source as well as difficulty. The acceptance gap holds within DeepScaleR problems, but events could
not be compared within one source. These limits restrict how far the claim generalizes. Within the
setting, the seeded dose was assigned at random through the Latin square and every exposure score was computed before its
run, which rules out post-hoc fitting.

\section*{Ethics statement}

The hacking patterns studied here were published before this work \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}. Our
results show which of them a policy trained against the judge exploits. The result helps practitioners screen judges before RL and helps
evaluators who rely on LLM judges, but it could also help someone game a judge-based evaluation. We report every
pattern and judge so that these weaknesses are known and testable. All data and models are used for research under
their licenses. xVerify-0.5B-I is licensed for non-commercial use and was used for inference only. The experiments used about 228 GPU hours.

\section{Statistical details and sensitivity analyses}
\label{app:stats}

\paragraph{H1.} AUROC counts ties as one half. Each of 10,000 bootstrap replicates resamples whole runs with
replacement within each judge. Replicates without an event are dropped, which happened once in 10,000 on the core
runs. Differences to each baseline use the same replicates and units, with the two-sided $p$-value
$2\min(P(d \le 0), P(d \ge 0))$. A $p$ of 0 enters the Holm correction as $10^{-4}$. On the held-out runs exposure leads FPR alone by 0.017 (0.000 to
0.035, $p = 0.066$).

\paragraph{Run-level checks.} Over the 12 unmasked runs with an event, the mean reciprocal rank of the first winner is 1.00
for exposure, 0.71 for FPR alone and 0.52 for propensity alone. The AUROC within each of these runs counts all its events as
positives. Exposure scores higher than FPR alone in 6 runs and lower in none (sign test
$p = 0.031$), and higher than propensity alone in 11 runs and lower in none ($p = 0.001$). Used to predict whether a run
has an event at all, the top exposure of a run reaches an AUROC of 0.865 over the 33 seeded runs and 0.981 over the
21 seeded hard-pool runs, against 0.815 and 0.944 for the top FPR. On the hard pool 12 of the 13 runs whose top
family reaches 1.92 had an event, and none of the 8 below it. The lowest exposure of a core event is 1.92 and that of
a held-out event 2.09.

\paragraph{Second events and event time.} In the two runs with a second event, the
second family ranks third (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct, seed 1) and second (R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B, seed 2) by
exposure among the 13 families left after the winner. Over the 14 events the event step has a Spearman correlation of $-0.84$ with exposure
(permutation $p = 0.0003$), $-0.43$ with FPR and $-0.52$ with propensity. The least-squares slope of log step on log
exposure is $-0.88$ over all events and $-1.03$ over the 12 first events. The kinetic growth time $\log(1/\phat)/(\fpr - q_0)$ correlates 0.73 with
the event step.

\paragraph{H2 and H3.} H2 is the exact McNemar test on the 12 pairs of core runs that share judge, checkpoint and seed.
The outcome of a run is whether some family has an event. The advantages of Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}b are
plug-in values at the mean acceptance rate, since the training logs keep only flagged responses. Over the 56 pairs of core judge and
family with at least 50 responses in each pool, the judges accepted 14.7\% of family responses on both pools in the
first 10 training steps. The hard pool is higher in 23 pairs and the easy pool in 22, and both rates stay close to
the audit FPR. For H3 the statistic is the sum over all core events of
the dose rank (1 to 3) that the event's family received. The null distribution is taken over all 252,252 ways of splitting
the 14 families into groups of five, five and four with outcomes held fixed. This test is exact under the null
hypothesis that the dose changes no outcome, including through competition within a run. With all events on four
families, the largest statistic the design allows is 26, which would give a two-sided $p$ of 0.017. The observed
statistic of 25 is the second largest (two-sided $p = 0.056$, one-sided 0.033).

\paragraph{Expected-advantage score.} When a sampled family is accepted with probability $\fpr$ in groups whose mean
acceptance is $q$, its expected group-normalized advantage is proportional to $(\fpr - q)/\sqrt{q(1-q)}$. We set $q$ to the judge's acceptance $q_0$ of the 200 step-0 evaluation outputs of a run, which come from the starting policy before any
update. Weighting this advantage by propensity gives the score $\phat\,(\fpr - q_0)/\sqrt{q_0(1-q_0)}$, which we
report as a secondary analysis outside the five main tests of Table~\ref{tab:tests}. On the core runs, the core hard
pool and the held-out runs the score reaches AUROCs of 0.993, 0.988 and 0.998, against 0.974, 0.984 and 0.995 for
exposure. The lead on the core runs is 0.019 over exposure (0.004 to 0.037, $p = 0.008$) and 0.056 over FPR alone
(0.030 to 0.082). All 12 winners are ranked first. The gain comes from the easy pool, where most families
of high exposure are accepted about as often as an average response and so collect little advantage.

\paragraph{Masking runs.} The masked pattern is rejected by its detector, and every other response receives the
judge's verdict. Two runs mask the main-run winner and two the top-FPR pattern. The two checkpoints were rebuilt from the
same clean pool and recipe. The rebuilt seeding sets match the originals, and the sampled propensity differs by at
most 0.0015. Predictions came from these checkpoints and from the timing fit of the main runs, and
they were committed before training. The fit is $\log t = 5.64 - 1.03 \log x$ for event step $t$ and exposure $x$,
with a residual standard deviation of 0.38. All four runs had an event. Exposure named the winner in three, at steps 60, 80 and 50
against predicted 55, 61 and 66 (bands 29 to 102, 33 to 114 and 36 to 124), so every step fell inside its band.
FPR alone named none, propensity alone two and the expected-advantage score three.
In the fourth run the winner ranks third by exposure and second by the expected-advantage score among
the unmasked patterns. Over the main and masked runs together, exposure ranks the first winner first in 15 of 16 runs. The exact McNemar
test gives 9 to 0
against FPR alone ($p = 0.004$) and 11 to 0 against propensity alone ($p = 0.001$), although a masked run and the
main run of its seed share their checkpoint.

\paragraph{Secondary analyses.} Log models use the Jeffreys-smoothed FPR $(k + 0.5)/(n + 1)$, since many FPRs are 0.
Censoring each family at the first event of another family in its run gives cause-specific hazards. A Cox model was fitted only where there are at least 10 events per covariate, which holds for the 12 first events of all 33 seeded runs and not for the 7 of the core runs alone. The winner conditional logit separates the data for the full and the product model, so
their likelihoods are suprema. Under separation the chi-square
approximation of the likelihood-ratio tests of FPR alone ($p = 1.1 \times 10^{-6}$) and propensity alone
($p = 1.4 \times 10^{-11}$) is unreliable, so Section~\ref{sec:results} compares the scores at the run level with
exact McNemar tests. Early growth is the change in a family's log share of the training responses from steps 0 to 9 to the 10
steps ending 10 steps before the run's first event, or to steps 40 to 49 in a run without one. Each share adds 0.5
to the family's count before the log is taken. The Spearman correlation of early growth is 0.28 with FPR, $-0.04$ with exposure and $-0.33$ with propensity, since seeded strings decay unless the judge accepts them.

\paragraph{Sensitivity analyses.} Each variant changes one part of the primary test and repeats it on the core runs
(Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). The event definition varies $\tau$ over half, once and twice the primary value, each with
one or two consecutive evaluations above the step-0 value. The looser definitions add single-evaluation rises of 5 to
13 outputs in 200. Propensity is replaced by the total log-likelihood of the family's canonical strings under the
fine-tuned policy, by their per-token log-likelihood or by the seeded dose, and the likelihoods are combined with FPR
in log space. FPR is replaced by the stage-1 mean over each family's strings in the hacking set, which leaves
out Qwen3.6 because stage 1 did not audit it. The last variant keeps only the hard pool. The lead of exposure over FPR alone stays between 0.033 and
0.041 except for per-token likelihood, which favors short repetitive strings and is not a frequency.
